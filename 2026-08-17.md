## Top 10

### 1. [Modular Cognitive Architecture Emerges in Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13567)

- Asks whether the human brain's functional specialization (distinct networks for language, formal reasoning, theory-of-mind, physical reasoning) is a biological accident or a general property of intelligent systems.
- Runs circuit analyses across 46 tasks spanning four cognitive domains on LLMs, a system trained by a completely different optimization process than evolution.
- Finds LLMs develop the same modular organization: tasks that share a network in the human brain recruit overlapping neurons in LLMs, while tasks from different human networks recruit distinct neurons.
- The convergent emergence of modularity in both brains and neural networks suggests it may be a fundamental property of intelligence rather than a quirk of biology — directly relevant to interpretability and the science of how capabilities organize internally.

### 2. [Amplified Does Not Mean Predictive: Reasoning Behaviors in Thinking Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13760)

- Asks whether reasoning-oriented training amplifies the specific chain-of-thought behaviors that actually predict correct answers, or just makes traces look more deliberative.
- Introduces "Behavioral Lift" to measure how much a behavior's presence changes correctness, and annotates 15,282 traces across 15 models and 6 benchmarks (text and vision-language).
- Finds an "Amplification-Lift Gap": training strongly amplifies self-correction, hypothesis testing, and uncertainty acknowledgment, but the behaviors most tied to correctness — confidence calibration, knowledge alignment, self-awareness — are barely amplified (and amplified uncertainty acknowledgment is only weakly/negatively linked to correctness).
- A clean example of training dynamics decoupling surface reasoning style from the substance that actually drives outcomes; motivates process-level objectives targeting calibration rather than surface form.

### 3. [Language-Specific Gaps in AI Safety Training Datasets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13695)

- Tests whether multilingual safety-benchmark "coverage" claims (a dozen+ languages) survive inspection at the level of individual languages, auditing 21 resources across low-, mid-, and high-resource language tiers (Hausa, Swahili, French).
- Finds recurring gaps in provenance, annotation reliability, harm-taxonomy coverage, and data reuse that only partially track resource level — e.g. one language slice fails its own paper's translation-quality bar while the same pipeline's output for another language clears it easily.
- Self-harm and sexual-content categories have zero native-language coverage in either African-language tier studied — a total, not gradated, gap.
- Connects these thin spots to the documented asymmetry where multilingual multi-turn jailbreaks still succeed even as single-turn attacks are mitigated, and contributes a reusable slice-level audit methodology.

### 4. [Think in Latent, Explain in Language: Self-Explainable Latent Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13570)

- Latent reasoning (compressing chain-of-thought into embeddings, à la Coconut) is efficient but opaque; existing fixes are either unexplainable black boxes or bolt on a separate post-hoc decoder.
- Proposes SELR: a single model trained with a joint objective — an Answer Loss for the latent trajectory and a CoT Loss that trains the same model to decode its own latent states back into human-readable reasoning steps.
- No external decoder needed; the same weights produce both the efficient latent computation and its own natural-language explanation.
- Shown to beat baselines on both token efficiency and accuracy on LLMs and VLMs, directly attacking the interpretability cost of efficient latent-space reasoning.

### 5. [Regime-Conditional Verification: Correctness Estimation for Adapting and Monitoring Safety Classifiers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14089)

- Safety classifiers deployed alongside LLMs fail for two reasons: they encode the training-time policy rather than the deployer's actual policy, and they degrade as traffic distribution shifts.
- Introduces RCV, a lightweight wrapper that reads a classifier's internal representations to estimate the probability a given prediction disagrees with the deployer's policy, and selectively corrects likely-wrong predictions — no retraining of the underlying classifier.
- The same correctness signal doubles as a label-free drift detector, enabling a maintenance loop that only escalates to full fine-tuning when lightweight correction stops working.
- Across three off-the-shelf classifiers and two datasets, catches up to 81% of previously missed unsafe content, and in a red-team study detects every one of ten held-out attack campaigns.

### 6. [Detecting Contaminated Code-Generation Prompt Batches via Influence Functions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14303)

- Targets prompts crafted to elicit insecure code from LLMs, where existing defenses rely on known vulnerability patterns and miss novel attacks.
- CodeSIFT uses influence functions to measure the parameter-space influence of generated code, then statistically tests whether a batch of prompts deviates from a benign reference distribution — threat-model-agnostic, no prior knowledge of the attack class needed.
- Achieves AUROC up to 0.98 across three open-weight code LLMs (3B–7B) at moderate-to-high injection rates, beating static-analysis baselines while staying well-calibrated.
- A concrete instance of using data-attribution machinery (influence functions) for a security/detection use case, rather than just training-data analysis.

### 7. [Capacity-Dependent Effects of Data Selection for Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13721)

- Re-examines the common wisdom that fine-tuning data closer to the student model's own likelihood distribution provides better supervision, using controlled distillation experiments with 1.5B–8B math-reasoning students and stronger teachers.
- Finds a capacity-dependent "Fast-Fit / Slow-Gain" pattern: high-likelihood data gives faster, more stable early gains (especially for smaller models), but low-likelihood data becomes increasingly valuable for larger models given enough training time.
- Explains the mechanism via learning dynamics — small models fail to absorb low-likelihood supervision and collapse into shallow/repetitive behavior, while larger models can move toward the teacher distribution.
- Offers a capacity-constrained theoretical account of distillation, arguing data selection should be conditioned on model capacity and compute budget rather than a single universal rule.

### 8. [When Does More Correct Data Hurt? Insertion-Stability and the Limits of Dimension-Based Theory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14020)

- Investigates the counterintuitive result that adding more *correctly labeled* training data can worsen worst-case learning guarantees under a monotone adversary that appends adversarially chosen (but correctly labeled) examples.
- Introduces "insertion-stability": a learner is immune to this adversary if more correct examples can only shrink its region of error — such learners keep the clean PAC rate regardless of what's appended.
- Shows classical complexity measures (VC dimension, Littlestone dimension) can't predict immunity: two classes can share the same VC/Littlestone dimension and still diverge sharply in adversarial robustness.
- A foundational learning-theory result about exactly when "more (correct) data" is actually safe — squarely in the science-of-generalization space.

### 9. [No Universal Signal Predicts Sample-Level LLM Regression under Version Updates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13607)

- Frontier LLM updates improve aggregate benchmarks but can silently flip individual samples from correct to incorrect — this paper studies whether inference-time signals can predict which samples will regress.
- Compares single-model signals (confidence, logit margin, attention entropy) against cross-version signals (output KL divergence, likelihood drift, representation drift) across six benchmarks, three task families, and six model-update pairs.
- Finds effectiveness is highly task- and update-dependent: confidence works best on MCQ/easy math, likelihood/KL signals win on harder math and code, and no signal dominates universally.
- Proposes a practical selective-fallback strategy that routes high-risk samples back to the old model when confidence-based detection fails — relevant to monitoring training/deployment dynamics over model lifecycles.

### 10. [A Four-Axis Trustworthiness Benchmark for LLM-as-Judge in Principle-Based Regulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14329)

- Argues LLM-as-judge, increasingly used to evaluate compliance with vague regulatory principles ("fair, clear, not misleading"), must be assessed on four axes at once: accuracy, paraphrase robustness, adversarial robustness, and calibration.
- Releases Principle-Bench (168 cryptoasset financial-promotion scenarios) with adversarial keyword-stuffing and paraphrase perturbations, plus Ceca, a calibrated assessor that emits per-exemplar counterfactual attributions.
- Finds no method dominates all four axes; a 120B LLM-judge that's strongest on benign inputs loses 47 accuracy points under keyword-stuffing ("compliance theatre"), and a second judge from a different model family agrees with it only weakly (Cohen's κ = 0.16) on that adversarial split.
- A sharp empirical warning about deploying LLM-as-judge for real regulatory/alignment evaluation without adversarial and calibration testing.

## Honorable Mentions

- **[A Calibrated Test of Internal Action Maps: State Signals Without Global Affine Closure](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13626)** — probes whether LLM hidden states support reusable, composable "action maps" beyond mere decodability, finding causal effects concentrated at specific late layers but no map that survives full compositional closure tests.
- **[Identifiability and Order-Dimension Limits of In-Context Learning on Partial Orders](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14004)** — a formal theory of in-context learning on partial orders (rather than functions), proving exact bounds on when a prompt logically determines a queried comparison versus leaving it genuinely ambiguous.
- **[The Integer Alibi: Localizing Cross-Kernel Divergence in INT8-Quantized LLM Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13756)** — a rigorous reproducibility study showing two "equivalent" INT8 GPU kernels for the same model disagree on every output sequence, localizing the cause to scale application and rounding rather than the (provably exact) integer accumulator.
- **[GRPO Beyond English: A Large-Scale Study of GRPO in Non-English and Multilingual Settings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13698)** — a large multilingual study of RLVR/GRPO training finding strong crosslingual transfer on average but model- and language-specific regressions on out-of-domain capabilities.
- **[You Only Pass Once: Answering and Abstaining Together in a Single Forward Pass of a Frozen Language Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14465)** — combines a steering probe that recovers reasoning accuracy and a sufficiency-detection direction that triggers abstention, both read from the same frozen residual stream in a single forward pass.
- **[AI Evaluation Should Work With Humans](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13577)** — a position paper arguing the field's focus on superhuman autonomous benchmarks misdirects development, and that evaluating human-AI teams would better target genuinely beneficial deployment.
- **[Don't Claim Benchmark-Oriented Optimization Improves General Coding Capability -- Diverse Evaluation Is Required](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13566)** — shows post-training optimized for SWE-bench/LiveCodeBench largely fails to transfer to a new Django-based benchmark, a cautionary generalization-of-evaluation result.
- **[Wrong but Useful: Trajectory Value Beyond Answer Correctness in Multi-Agent Messages](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14375)** — shows that in multi-agent LLM deliberation, messages with wrong final answers frequently still improve downstream reasoning, decoupling "message correctness" from "message usefulness" as filtering criteria.
